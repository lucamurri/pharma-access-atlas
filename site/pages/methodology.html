Methodology & sources

How the atlas is built

Every number on this site comes from a public source and links to it. Where a value could not be verified, it is left blank and flagged. Nothing is estimated.

Which drugs are included

Every medicine that meets a fixed rule, with no hand-picking: the FDA approved it as a new active substance between 1 January 2019 and 31 December 2025 (an original NDA classed as a new molecular entity, or an original BLA, in openFDA), and the European Commission's first centralised authorisation of the same active substance also falls in that window. That gives {{n}} drugs.

  • Generics, biosimilars and new formulations of older molecules drop out automatically, because their substance was first authorised in the EU before 2019.
  • Diagnostic agents (ATC V04, V09) are excluded: they are not treatments and are reimbursed through other routes.
  • Cell and gene therapies (approved by FDA's CBER, not in openFDA) come from the EMA list of advanced therapies. Each is included only if its FDA approval date in the window was verified by hand.
  • The selection was cross-checked against FDA's official yearly "Novel Drug Approvals" lists for 2019–2025 (FDA no longer hosts the 2019 and 2020 pages, so Internet Archive copies were used). Every drug on those lists is either in the atlas or left out for a stated reason: no EU central authorisation, a first EU authorisation outside 2019–2025, or a diagnostic agent. The check found four drugs a first version of the selection had missed, because of how their substance is written in the EMA file (Mayzent, Rukobia, Vocabria) or because the drug was withdrawn in the US and removed from openFDA (Pepaxti, approved by the FDA as Pepaxto); they are included. One drug in the atlas is not on FDA's lists: Nustendi (bempedoic acid with ezetimibe), because FDA counts bempedoic acid once, under Nexletol. The check is pipeline/crosscheck_fda.py.
  • The window cuts off recent drugs with long gaps. Both approvals must fall in 2019–2025, so a drug the FDA approved in 2024–2025 and the EU only from 2026 is not in the atlas. Among recent approvals, only those Europe approved quickly are included, so the median US-to-EU gap of {{lag_median}} days may understate the gap. Finding 2 leaves those years out for this reason.
  • {{n_reference}} reference drugs (Rybelsus and Wegovy, newer brands of semaglutide, which was first approved as Ozempic in 2017) are shown for the Medicare comparison but excluded from every headline statistic.
  • The EU side uses centrally authorised products. A substance previously sold only under national licences can appear "new" to the EU here. The selection code is pipeline/universe.py.

Therapy areas come from each drug's ATC code (WHO classification, as published by the EMA) through fixed rules in pipeline/taxonomy.py. Where no ATC code is assigned yet, keywords in the EMA therapeutic area are used. Orphan status is a separate filter, not a therapy area. Company is the EU marketing authorisation holder with legal-form words removed.

Definitions

FDA approval date
Approval date of the drug's original application (NDA or BLA) in Drugs@FDA. Later approvals for new indications are ignored. For cell and gene therapies (regulated by FDA's CBER and absent from openFDA), the date of the original BLA approval letter.
EC approval date
Date of the European Commission's initial marketing authorisation, as published by the EMA.
US-to-EU approval lag
EC approval date minus FDA approval date, in days. Negative means the EU approved first.
Italy: time to national reimbursement
Days from EC approval to publication in the Gazzetta Ufficiale of the first AIFA determination granting reimbursement by the national health service (class A or H). Classes C and C(nn) mean the drug may be sold but is not reimbursed. Drugs still not reimbursed are shown as “waiting” with the days elapsed up to the date we checked. Headline medians count them (see time to access); the therapy-area chart uses reimbursed drugs only and says so.
Italy: AIFA innovativeness
Whether the reimbursement determination grants AIFA's innovativeness status: full, conditional, or not recognised when the determination grants none. It is read from the determination that grants reimbursement, so a status granted later (for example for a new indication) is not counted, and drugs not yet reimbursed have none. Full innovativeness gives access to the innovative-medicines fund and, by law, to regional formularies without waiting for regional inclusion (art. 10, c. 2, Decree-Law 158/2012). It is Italy's own judgement on the drug, made with its own criteria; it is not on the same scale as the G-BA, HAS or NICE verdicts, and the atlas does not score them against each other.
Italy: AIFA procedure dates
The reimbursement determination lists the steps before it: the company's application, the opinion of AIFA's scientific committee (CTS), the opinion of the price and reimbursement committee (CPR), which sets the price agreed for reimbursement, and the resolution of AIFA's Board (CdA). From 2024 a single scientific and economic committee (CSE) replaced the CTS and CPR. Where a committee met over several days, the last day is used.
Germany G-BA rating
The result of the drug's first concluded early benefit assessment (AMNOG, § 35a SGB V). A decision can split patients into groups with different ratings; we show the best rating across groups and flag whether any group got “no added benefit”. In Germany a drug is reimbursed from launch. The assessment, usually about six months later, shapes the negotiated price, so it measures value recognised, not time to reimbursement. "No added benefit" is relative to the comparator the G-BA chose for that patient group; it does not mean the drug has no clinical value. "Non-quantifiable" means the G-BA found an added benefit but the data did not allow it to set its size; {{gba_n_nq_orphan}} of the {{gba_n_nq}} drugs rated this way are orphan drugs, whose added benefit the law deems proven.
France HAS rating
The HAS Transparency Committee's first opinion on inclusion for reimbursement. SMR says whether the drug merits reimbursement at all; ASMR rates added benefit from I (major) to V (no improvement). We show the best level across indications. When the SMR is insufficient for every indication, HAS gives no ASMR; we show that as "SMR insufficient" and count it, with ASMR V, as a negative verdict. Values come from the French public medicines database. For drugs missing there (often because they were never marketed in France), the opinion was researched on has-sante.fr; where HAS has no inclusion opinion, the drug counts as "not yet" on the date checked.
England NICE decision
The outcome of NICE's first published technology appraisal (TA) or highly specialised technology (HST) guidance for the drug's first indication. Categories: recommended within the licence; optimised (recommended for a narrower group); managed access (Cancer Drugs Fund or similar); not recommended; terminated (no company submission or the company withdrew); and not appraised (no guidance, including appraisals discontinued before guidance was published). The date is the publication date. England uses its own UK licence after Brexit, but lags are measured from the EC approval for comparability. A terminated appraisal ({{en_n_term}} drugs) counts as reaching the milestone on its publication date, because NICE publishes it as guidance and it settles the outcome: the NHS does not fund the drug for that indication. If terminated appraisals were counted instead as not yet reached, the English median would be {{en_med_cens}} days instead of {{en_med_all}}, and the share reached within two years {{en_2y_cens}}% instead of {{en_2y_all}}%.
Orphan status
US: an FDA orphan designation for the indication of first approval (FDA OOPD database). EU: the EMA's current orphan flag. A drug whose orphan status was later withdrawn shows “no”.
Medicare negotiation
Whether CMS selected the drug under the Inflation Reduction Act, and if so the Maximum Fair Price (MFP) and the list price that CMS reports for a 30-day supply.

Drugs without a verdict

Shares of verdicts (for example "no added benefit" or "ASMR V") are of the drugs that have one, not of all {{n}}.

  • Germany: {{gba_n_assessed}} drugs have a G-BA rating. Of the other {{gba_none_n}}, {{gba_none_na}} have no G-BA procedure on record (the reason is not recorded), {{gba_none_ongoing}} have an assessment still in progress and {{gba_none_unk}} could not be verified.
  • France: {{fr_n}} drugs have a HAS inclusion opinion. The other {{fr_none}} have none, often because the drug was never marketed in France.
  • Italy: {{it_n_reimbursed}} drugs are reimbursed and {{it_n_waiting}} were not reimbursed when checked. For the other {{it_n_unknown}}, the Italian status could not be verified from public sources, so they are left out of the Italian curve and medians.
  • England: {{en_n}} drugs have a NICE decision (recommended, optimised, managed access, not recommended or terminated). Of the other {{en_none_n}}, {{en_none_prog}} have an appraisal in development, {{en_none_na}} will not be appraised and {{en_none_unk}} could not be verified.

Time to access: counting drugs still waiting

A median taken only over drugs that already reached a milestone leaves out the drugs still waiting, and those take the longest. It understates the time, and the more recent the drugs, the worse the bias. The headline medians and the country chart therefore use the Kaplan–Meier method, the standard way to measure time until an event when some cases have not had it yet:

  • A drug with a dated milestone (reimbursement, G-BA decision, HAS opinion or NICE guidance) is counted on that day.
  • A drug confirmed as not there yet (for example "not reimbursed as of", NICE appraisal "in development", G-BA procedure ongoing, or no procedure or opinion at all) stays in the count until the day it was checked, then leaves it without an event.
  • A drug never checked (status unknown) is left out.

The curve shows the share of drugs that had reached the milestone by each day after EC approval, and the median is the first day it reaches 50%. Drugs NICE will never appraise keep the English curve below 100%, which is accurate: they never get NICE guidance. The English curve starts above 0% because NICE, working from the UK licence, published guidance for a few drugs before the EC approval.

Robustness: early and recent approvals

Many drugs in the atlas were approved recently and are still waiting, so the curves lean on them. To check whether the headline medians depend on the mix of years, the curves were redrawn separately for drugs with EC approval in 2019–2021 and in 2022–2025. The differences are small and none is statistically significant (log-rank test), so the overall curves are not driven by one period. The recent group has had less time to reach each milestone, so its two-year shares rest on fewer drugs.

Italy measured at the price opinion

Italy's milestone, publication in the Gazzetta Ufficiale, comes after the price has been agreed and after the administrative steps that follow. As an additional analysis, without changing the main comparison, the Italian curve was also drawn to the date of AIFA's price opinion (CPR, or CSE from 2024), read from each determination. Counted that way, half of drugs had a price opinion within {{it_price_med}} days of EU approval ({{it_price_n}} drugs), against {{it_med}} days to publication. For the {{it_admin_n}} drugs with both dates, the time from the price opinion to publication had a median of {{it_admin_med}} days, and half of drugs fell between {{it_admin_p25}} and {{it_admin_p75}} days. Drugs not yet reimbursed count as not having reached the price opinion on the day they were checked; some may already have one, so this curve may slightly understate how quickly price opinions come.

Comparing groups of drugs

Finding 7 splits the drugs by one trait at a time (for example, orphan or not) and compares the two groups' curves with a log-rank test, the standard test for this kind of curve. The test compares the whole curves; the chart shows only the share reached at two years, with 95% confidence intervals (Greenwood's method, log-log transform) in the table. The p-value is the probability of seeing a gap at least as large as the one observed if there were no real difference between the groups. It is not the probability that the gap is due to chance. With several comparisons, one result below 0.05 can appear even if no trait matters, so we also give the Bonferroni threshold (0.05 divided by the number of comparisons). With small groups, a result that is not significant does not rule out an effect. None of the comparisons shows cause: a trait can travel with others (large companies run more oncology drugs, for example). The calculation was checked against the lifelines statistics library.

Quality checks

An automated validation step runs before every publication and stops the build if any non-blank value lacks a source link and retrieval date, if dates are illogical (for example, Italian reimbursement before EU approval), or if a drug appears twice. The pipeline code and every input file are public on GitHub.

What is and isn't covered

  • Prices are mostly out of scope. US list prices (WAC) are not freely available, and European net prices are confidential. The only prices shown are those CMS publishes for drugs selected for Medicare negotiation ({{cms_n_selected}} of the {{n_all}} drugs shown, both of them reference drugs outside the {{n}}-drug statistics).
  • Few drugs here can be negotiated yet. By law a drug becomes eligible 7 years after approval (11 for biologics). Rybelsus and Wegovy are included only because CMS grouped all semaglutide products with Ozempic (approved 2017).
  • Early access in Italy is not counted. Payment under Law 648/96 or the AIFA 5% fund can precede full reimbursement (for example Zolgensma and Skyclarys). We count only the reimbursement determination.
  • Regional access in Italy (inclusion in regional formularies) can add months after national reimbursement and is not measured.
  • Germany, France and England measure value recognised; Italy measures reimbursement. In Germany a drug is reimbursed from launch; in France and England the assessment precedes funding. Only Italy's milestone is reimbursement itself.
  • Reserve antibiotics in Germany have their added benefit "deemed proven" without a stated extent, so their rating is left blank.
  • The COVID-19 pandemic falls within the study window. In 2020–2021 regulators and assessment bodies reprioritised their work, which may have changed timelines. The atlas does not adjust for it.
  • Recent drugs are incomplete. Many drugs approved in 2024–2025 are still in assessment; they show as waiting or unknown, never as "no".
  • Researched values for Italy, Germany, France (where missing from the BDPM), England and US orphan status were researched from official sources under strict rules (an official source for every value, no estimates) and spot-checked. Errors are possible: every value links to its source so it can be checked.
  • US access depends on insurers. FDA approval does not guarantee coverage. US payer coverage decisions are not public in a comparable form and are not measured.
  • Judgement calls (for example a suspended first G-BA procedure, or a drug first classed as not reimbursed) are documented next to each value in the source tables on every drug brief.
