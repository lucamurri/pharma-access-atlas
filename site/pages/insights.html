Seven findings on getting new drugs to patients

These findings cover all {{n}} new medicines approved by both the FDA and the European Commission in 2019–2025. Each one states how many drugs it rests on. Recent approvals are still moving through national processes, so some values are not yet known.

1 Approval is the short part of the wait, and Italy is the slowest step

EU approval came a median {{lag_median}} days after the FDA's. Then national processes take over, and in Italy that step alone takes more than twice as long as the regulatory gap. These medians count the drugs still waiting (a time-to-access curve, see the methodology), so slow cases are not left out. Counted from EU approval, half of drugs had a French HAS opinion within {{fr_med}} days ({{fr_n2}} drugs, {{fr_wait}} still waiting), a German G-BA decision within {{de_med}} ({{de_n2}}, {{de_wait}} waiting) and English NICE guidance within {{en_med}} ({{en_n2}}, {{en_wait}} waiting). Italian reimbursement, by contrast, took {{it_med}} days to reach half of drugs ({{it_n2}} drugs, {{it_wait}} still waiting), and two years after EU approval only {{it_2y}}% of drugs were reimbursed. End to end, it took {{fda_to_it_median}} days, over two years, from US approval until half of drugs were reimbursed in Italy.

So what: the pricing and reimbursement process, not the regulator, is the critical path for European launch. Plan it, and fund it, as early as the regulatory filing.

2 Europe's regulatory gap is closing

For drugs the FDA approved in 2019, EU approval followed a median {{lag19}} days later ({{lag19_n}} drugs). For 2022 approvals the gap was {{lag22}} days ({{lag22_n}} drugs). Later years are left out of this comparison on purpose. A drug the FDA approved in 2024–2025 only enters the atlas if the EU also approved it by the end of 2025, so their gaps are cut short by the study window. {{lag_eu_first}} of the {{n}} drugs were approved in Europe first.

So what: simultaneous US and EU filing is becoming the norm. The competitive advantage now lies in what happens after approval.

3 Without orphan protection, most drugs fail to show added benefit in Germany

Of the {{comp_n}} drugs Germany's G-BA compared against an existing therapy, {{comp_nab_n}} ({{comp_nab_pct}}%) showed no added benefit for any patient group in the first decision. All {{orph_route_n}} drugs assessed under the orphan rules received a benefit rating, because the law deems it proven.

So what: a positive German rating requires head-to-head evidence against the German comparator. Global trial programmes designed around the FDA's endpoints often do not provide it.

4 England often funds what Germany found no added benefit for

Among the {{de_nab_en_n}} drugs that Germany found had no added benefit and that NICE also appraised, {{de_nab_en_pos_n}} ({{de_nab_en_pos_pct}}%) received a positive NICE decision: recommended, recommended for a narrower group, or funded through managed access. The two bodies ask different questions. G-BA measures added benefit over a comparator, while NICE judges value for money at the (often confidential) price offered.

So what: a negative German verdict is not the end of the European story. In England, price is the lever that can turn a "no" into a "yes".

5 Germany and France disagree on more than a third of drugs

For the {{both_n}} drugs with both a German rating and a French verdict, the two agencies reached opposite verdicts on {{disagree_n}} ({{disagree_pct}}%): one found added benefit where the other found none ("no added benefit" in Germany; ASMR V, or a benefit too small to reimburse at all, in France). They agreed on {{agree_pos_n}} drugs having added benefit and on {{agree_neg_n}} drugs having none.

So what: "European HTA" is not one judgement. The EU's joint clinical assessment, which began in 2025, will test how far these views can converge.

6 One molecule, many deals: semaglutide

CMS negotiated a single Medicare price for all semaglutide products: ${{cms_mfp}} per 30-day supply from {{cms_ipay}}, {{cms_discount}}% below the ${{cms_list}} list price CMS reports for {{cms_list_year}}. In Italy, oral Rybelsus for diabetes was reimbursed {{ryb_it}} days after EU approval, but Wegovy for weight management was still unreimbursed {{weg_waiting}} days after it. Germany found no added benefit for Rybelsus and has no benefit assessment for Wegovy, which statutory insurance excludes as a "lifestyle" medicine. (Rybelsus and Wegovy are reference drugs, not part of the {{n}}-drug statistics.)

So what: the same molecule can be negotiated, reimbursed or excluded depending on the indication and the country. For obesity, payer policy, more than the evidence, decides access.

7 In Italy, the process sets the pace, not the drug

We split the drugs {{drv_n}} ways (orphan or not, Germany's verdict, France's verdict, conditional approval, cell or gene therapy, oncology, company size) and compared how fast each group was reimbursed in Italy, counting drugs still waiting. Every group's median fell between {{drv_med_lo}} and {{drv_med_hi}} days. Drugs Germany found had added benefit took {{drv_de_y_med}} days ({{drv_de_y_n}} drugs), against {{drv_de_n_med}} for those it found had none ({{drv_de_n_n}}). Orphan drugs took {{drv_or_y_med}}, others {{drv_or_n_med}}. None of these gaps passes a standard significance test (p < 0.05).

The one factor that stood out was the company. Companies with at least five drugs in the atlas had {{drv_lc_y_2y}}% of their drugs reimbursed within two years of EU approval ({{drv_lc_y_n}} drugs), against {{drv_lc_n_2y}}% for smaller companies ({{drv_lc_n_n}}). A log-rank test puts the chance of a gap this large arising by luck at p = {{drv_lc_p}}; with {{drv_n}} comparisons, one such result could still be chance.

So what: a strong benefit verdict elsewhere does not buy a faster Italian launch. What seems to matter is how well a company works the Italian process: a local market-access team, early dossier preparation and price negotiation capacity.
