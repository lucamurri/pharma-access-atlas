Seven findings on getting new drugs to patients

These findings cover all {{n}} new medicines approved by both the FDA and the European Commission in 2019–2025. Each one states how many drugs it rests on. Recent approvals are still moving through national processes, so some values are not yet known.

Three different things. These findings measure the value recognised by assessment bodies (G-BA, HAS, NICE) and reimbursement (in Italy, publication in the Gazzetta Ufficiale). They do not measure actual availability to patients, which also depends on regional formularies and early-access routes such as Law 648/96 and the AIFA 5% fund. The four countries' milestones are different events (what each one measures), so their times describe different processes, not a ranking.

1 After EU approval, four countries reach four different milestones

EU approval came a median {{lag_median}} days after the FDA's. Then national processes take over. These medians count the drugs still waiting (a time-to-access curve, see the methodology), so slow cases are not left out. Counted from EU approval, half of drugs had a French HAS opinion within {{fr_med}} days ({{fr_n2}} drugs, {{fr_wait}} still waiting), a German G-BA decision within {{de_med}} ({{de_n2}}, {{de_wait}} waiting) and English NICE guidance within {{en_med}} ({{en_n2}}, {{en_wait}} waiting). Half of drugs were reimbursed in Italy within {{it_med}} days ({{it_n2}} drugs, {{it_wait}} still waiting), and {{it_2y}}% within two years of EU approval. Counted from US approval, half of drugs were reimbursed in Italy within {{fda_to_it_median}} days.

Reading this: the four milestones are not the same event. In Germany the drug is already reimbursed when the G-BA decides; in France and England the opinion comes before funding; in Italy the date measured comes after the price has been agreed. The times describe each process; they are not a ranking of countries.

2 Europe's regulatory gap is closing

For drugs the FDA approved in 2019, EU approval followed a median {{lag19}} days later ({{lag19_n}} drugs). For 2022 approvals the gap was {{lag22}} days ({{lag22_n}} drugs). Later years are left out of this comparison on purpose. A drug the FDA approved in 2024–2025 only enters the atlas if the EU also approved it by the end of 2025, so their gaps are cut short by the study window. {{lag_eu_first}} of the {{n}} drugs were approved in Europe first.

Reading this: the comparison rests on two cohorts, and these years overlap with the COVID-19 pandemic, which changed regulators' workloads. The data show the gap, not why it changed.

3 Without the orphan rules, most drugs were rated as having no added benefit in Germany

Of the {{comp_n}} drugs Germany's G-BA compared against an existing therapy, {{comp_nab_n}} ({{comp_nab_pct}}%) showed no added benefit for any patient group in the first decision. All {{orph_route_n}} drugs assessed under the orphan rules received a benefit rating, because the law deems it proven.

Reading this: "no added benefit" is relative to the comparator the G-BA chose; it does not mean the drug has no clinical value. Orphan drugs receive a rating by law, so the two routes are not directly comparable.

4 England often funds what Germany found no added benefit for

Among the {{de_nab_en_n}} drugs that Germany found had no added benefit and that NICE also appraised, {{de_nab_en_pos_n}} ({{de_nab_en_pos_pct}}%) received a positive NICE decision: recommended, recommended for a narrower group, or funded through managed access. The two bodies ask different questions. G-BA measures added benefit over a comparator, while NICE judges value for money at the (often confidential) price offered.

Reading this: the two verdicts answer different questions, so they do not contradict each other. NICE decisions often rest on a confidential price, which the atlas cannot see.

5 Germany and France disagree on more than a third of drugs

For the {{both_n}} drugs with both a German rating and a French verdict, the two agencies reached opposite verdicts on {{disagree_n}} ({{disagree_pct}}%): one found added benefit where the other found none ("no added benefit" in Germany; ASMR V, or a benefit too small to reimburse at all, in France). They agreed on {{agree_pos_n}} drugs having added benefit and on {{agree_neg_n}} drugs having none.

Reading this: the agencies use different criteria, comparators and scales, so different verdicts on the same drug are expected and are not an error by either. The EU's joint clinical assessment, which began in 2025, applies to medicines filed from then on.

6 One molecule, many deals: semaglutide

CMS negotiated a single Medicare price for all semaglutide products: ${{cms_mfp}} per 30-day supply from {{cms_ipay}}, {{cms_discount}}% below the ${{cms_list}} list price CMS reports for {{cms_list_year}}. In Italy, oral Rybelsus for diabetes was reimbursed {{ryb_it}} days after EU approval, but Wegovy for weight management was still unreimbursed {{weg_waiting}} days after it. Germany found no added benefit for Rybelsus and has no benefit assessment for Wegovy, which statutory insurance excludes as a "lifestyle" medicine. (Rybelsus and Wegovy are reference drugs, not part of the {{n}}-drug statistics.)

Reading this: each system applies its own rules to each indication, so the same molecule can be negotiated, reimbursed or excluded. The case shows how far outcomes can differ, not which is right.

7 Italian reimbursement times show no clear link to the traits measured here

We split the drugs {{drv_n}} ways (orphan or not, Germany's verdict, France's verdict, conditional approval, cell or gene therapy, oncology, company size) and compared how fast each group was reimbursed in Italy, counting drugs still waiting. Every group's median fell between {{drv_med_lo}} and {{drv_med_hi}} days. Drugs Germany found had added benefit took {{drv_de_y_med}} days ({{drv_de_y_n}} drugs), against {{drv_de_n_med}} for those it found had none ({{drv_de_n_n}}). Orphan drugs took {{drv_or_y_med}}, others {{drv_or_n_med}}. None of these gaps is statistically significant.

One comparison gave a p-value below 0.05. Companies with at least five drugs in the atlas had {{drv_lc_y_2y}}% of their drugs reimbursed within two years of EU approval ({{drv_lc_y_n}} drugs), against {{drv_lc_n_2y}}% for other companies ({{drv_lc_n_n}}); log-rank p = {{drv_lc_p}}. The p-value is the probability of seeing a gap at least this large if there were no real difference. With {{drv_n}} comparisons, a simple Bonferroni correction lowers the threshold to 0.05/{{drv_n}} ≈ {{drv_alpha}}, and no comparison passes it. "Company with five or more drugs" also stands for size, therapy-area mix and the priority a company gives Italy, none of which is measured.

The log-rank test compares the two groups' whole curves, while the bars below show only the share reached at two years. The table gives every comparison's p-value, group sizes and 95% confidence intervals.

Open question: the data show no clear relation between verdicts abroad and Italian reimbursement times. Many factors, such as how a company is organised, when it submits its dossier and how the price negotiation goes, are not observed. With groups this small, a result that is not significant does not rule out an effect.
