Six findings on getting new drugs to patients

Drawn only from the {{n}} drugs in the atlas. The sample is curated and small, so read these as patterns in this set of medicines, not laws of the market. Each finding states how many drugs it rests on.

1 Approval is the short part of the wait: Italian reimbursement takes more than twice as long as the US-to-EU gap

Across the set, EU approval came a median {{lag_median}} days after FDA approval. Reimbursement in Italy then took a median {{it_lag_median}} days more, counted to publication in the Gazzetta Ufficiale. End to end, reimbursed access in Italy came a median {{fda_to_it_median}} days (over two years) after the American approval ({{it_n}} reimbursed drugs). This understates the wait: {{it_n_waiting}} drugs ({{it_waiting_names}}) were still not reimbursed when checked.

So what: for European launch planning, the pricing and reimbursement process, not the regulator, is the critical path. It deserves the same early investment as the regulatory filing.

2 Orphan status is not a fast track to Italian reimbursement

Drugs with an orphan designation in the US or EU were reimbursed in Italy a median {{orph_it}} days after EU approval ({{orph_n}} drugs), essentially the same as the {{non_it}} days for non-orphan drugs ({{non_n}} drugs). Drugs holding EU orphan status did somewhat better, at a median {{eu_orph_it}} days ({{eu_orph_n}} drugs) versus {{eu_non_it}} ({{eu_non_n}} drugs), roughly {{eu_orph_gap}} days faster. That gap is small relative to the spread, and the sample is small.

So what: rarity alone does not shorten negotiations in Italy. The longest Italian wait in the atlas belongs to an orphan cell therapy, Carvykti ({{carvykti_it}} days), while the orphan gene therapy Zolgensma was among the fastest ({{zolgensma_it}} days).

3 Germany's verdict depends heavily on the assessment route

Overall, {{gba_n_nab}} of {{gba_n_assessed}} drugs showed no added benefit for any patient group in their first G-BA decision. The split by route is stark. Among the {{comp_n}} drugs compared against an existing therapy, {{comp_nab_n}} ({{comp_nab_pct}}%) showed no added benefit. All {{orphan_route_n}} drugs assessed under the orphan rules received a benefit rating, because the law deems their added benefit proven. In most cases G-BA rated it “non-quantifiable”.

So what: without orphan protection, a positive German rating requires head-to-head evidence against the local standard of care. Trial design for Europe has to anticipate the G-BA comparator, not only the FDA's endpoints.

4 Drugs Germany valued most also reached Italian patients faster

Drugs rated as offering major or considerable added benefit in Germany were reimbursed in Italy a median {{high_it}} days after EU approval ({{high_n}} drugs). Drugs with no added benefit took {{nab_it}} days ({{nab_n}} drugs), about {{high_gap}} days longer. The two countries decide independently, so this is an association, not cause and effect. It suggests that the strength of the evidence package shows up in both systems.

So what: a weak German result is an early warning sign for negotiations elsewhere in Europe, including Italy.

5 Oncology and cardiometabolic drugs face the longest Italian waits; conditional approvals wait longest of all

By therapy area, rare-disease drugs were reimbursed fastest in Italy (median {{rare_it}} days, {{rare_n}} drugs). Oncology ({{onc_it}} days, {{onc_n}} drugs) and cardiometabolic ({{cardio_it}} days, {{cardio_n}} drugs) were slowest. The {{cond_n}} drugs with an EU conditional approval waited a median {{cond_it}} days, against {{std_it}} for standard approvals ({{std_n}} drugs).

So what: Italian launch plans for oncology and cardiometabolic drugs, and for any conditionally approved drug, should assume a wait well beyond the overall median of {{it_lag_median}} days.

6 One molecule, three deals: semaglutide

CMS negotiated a single Medicare price for all semaglutide products (Ozempic, Rybelsus and Wegovy): ${{cms_mfp}} per 30-day supply from {{cms_ipay}}, {{cms_discount}}% below the ${{cms_list}} list price that CMS reports for {{cms_list_year}}. In Italy, oral Rybelsus for diabetes was reimbursed (class A) {{ryb_it}} days after EU approval. Wegovy for weight management remains in class C, unreimbursed {{weg_waiting}} days after EU approval. Germany's G-BA found no added benefit for Rybelsus. It has no benefit assessment for Wegovy, which statutory insurance excludes as a “lifestyle” medicine.

So what: the same active ingredient can be negotiated, reimbursed or excluded depending on the indication and the country. For obesity, payer policy, more than clinical evidence, is the gatekeeper in Europe.
